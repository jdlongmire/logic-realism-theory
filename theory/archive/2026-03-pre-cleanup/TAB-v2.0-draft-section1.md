## 1. Three Guiding Observations

The present argument begins from three observations about the structure of physical reality.

### 1.1 Physical Reality Has an Origin

The universe cannot be self-explanatory. The claim that the totality of what exists is simply brute and underived is unstable: even the denial that reality has an origin presupposes some background structure in virtue of which the denial is meaningful. The origin need not be temporal; it may be ontological. But the question arises: what is the minimal "something" in virtue of which there is anything at all?

Attempts to derive existence from absolute nothing face an immediate dilemma. Either "nothing" is truly absolute, in which case no derivation is possible, or "nothing" is qualified (a vacuum, a field, a potential), in which case something already exists and the question is merely deferred. Absolute nothing—the complete absence of being, structure, constraint, and possibility—cannot generate or ground existence. This is not merely an empirical observation but a logical necessity: nothing has no properties, including the property of being able to produce something.

The project of fundamental ontology therefore cannot begin with nothing. It must identify the minimal structure presupposed by any possible existence whatever.

### 1.2 Physical Reality Is Logical, Informational, and Dynamic

Our physical theories presuppose logical constraint: certain configurations are excluded as impossible. They describe systems through structured distinctions—states, fields, amplitudes—that are naturally interpreted as informational. And they encode change: systems evolve, transition, and actualize states.

These features suggest that any adequate primitive ontology must include:

- **Logical constraint:** a principle determining what can and cannot obtain
- **Informational possibility:** a domain of distinguishable configurations
- **Actualization:** a principle by which some configurations obtain while others remain merely possible

### 1.3 No Actuality Violates the Fundamental Laws of Logic

Apparent anomalies in physics never license genuine contradiction. Superposition, contextuality, and nonlocal correlations challenge classical intuitions, but they do not instantiate violations of identity, non-contradiction, or excluded middle. The working assumption of physics is that whatever is physically actual must be logically admissible.

This observation has direct ontological force. If actuality could violate logic, the very concept of determinate existence would collapse. An inconsistent state is not a state that could obtain but happens not to; it is not a candidate for obtaining at all.

### 1.4 The Primitive Ontology and the Two Core Results

These observations motivate the structure of the argument that follows.

From the first observation, we are led to seek a primitive ontological unity—the minimal "something" in virtue of which anything exists. We designate this unity as X.

From the second observation, X decomposes into three mutually constitutive elements:

$$X \equiv [L_3 : I_\infty : A]$$

where L₃ is prescriptive logical constraint, I∞ is the total informational possibility space, and A is the actualization primitive.

From the third observation, we derive the bridge constraint that governs the structure of what obtains:

$$A_\Omega = L_3(I_\infty)$$

The actualized domain coincides with the logically admissible configurations of the informational domain.

These two equations—the primitive unity and the bridge identity—are the core results of this paper. The sections that follow argue for each component in turn.

### 1.5 The Transcendental Method

Our approach is transcendental in the Kantian sense: we ask what conditions must obtain for any determinate existence to be possible. The answer to this question is not empirical but necessary. We are not asking what exists but what must be the case for existence to be determinate rather than chaotic, structured rather than formless.

The criterion for success is not that we derive the furniture of the world from pure reason. The criterion is that we identify primitives that cannot be coherently denied without presupposing them. Any account of reality that purports to dispense with these primitives will, upon examination, depend upon them.

The method requires precision about what "necessity" means in this context. We are not claiming causal necessity (as if the primitives caused reality to exist) or modal necessity in the possible-worlds sense. We are claiming constitutive necessity: these primitives are what it is for anything to be determinately actual.
